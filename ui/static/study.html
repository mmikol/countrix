The study

%(STATUS)s

Countrix picks a team of six with one formula and one search. This page asks two questions of them. Does the search return the best team its formula allows, or a good guess? And how close do its teams come to what players pick and to what wins, while they carry synergy, answer the other side and keep the playbook's rules? The first is answered by a proof, checked against brute force. The second is answered by a study: Countrix's teams beside the kinds of tool people use online - CounterWatch's team builder, tier lists built from the rates, counter pickers and the most-picked heroes - every tool's six scored on the same boards, on Countrix's own scale and on CounterWatch's 6v6 numbers, which Countrix as shipped never reads. A section between them asks whether anything in the engine is written for Overwatch alone.

In plain words

  • One formula scores every team. Countrix scores each team of six with the same formula. The default engine comes first: each pick's win rate on the map, the wiki's synergy among the six, and its counters to the other side. The playbook's heuristics then add to that score or take from it, and its limits decide which teams are allowed at all.
  • The claim. For any board and any playbook, the team Countrix calls optimal is the highest-scoring allowed team of all of them - not a good guess, the best. On a board with nothing picked or banned that is the best of over thirteen million teams (the count check below).
  • How it knows without scoring them all. The search groups the teams into branches: all the teams that share the picks made so far. For each branch it works out a safe overestimate, a number no team in the branch can beat. Once that number falls below the teams already kept, the whole branch is dropped. Every drop rests on arithmetic, never on a hunch.
  • How much work that takes. On an open board the search scores a few dozen teams in full at most and proves that none of the others can place, in hundredths of a second (the evidence).
  • It never guesses. If it cannot prove its answer within its budget - %(NODE_BUDGET)s branches or %(SCORE_BUDGET)s teams scored in full - it refuses. If no team is allowed, it proves that too.
  • It is repeatable. The same board, playbook and weights give the same team in any process. Two scores that agree to %(SCORE_PLACES)s decimal places are a tie, settled by a fixed number per hero: a hash of the map, the side and the hero, which never reads a hero's rates or name.
  • The check. A brute force scores every allowed team of a real board, one by one, for minutes; the search answers in hundredths of a second; and the two agree on the best teams to the last bit (the evidence).
  • What optimal means. Best under the scoring formula, given the playbook's assumptions - players play their heroes well, Open Queue 6v6, console rates and the rest (the assumptions). It is not a proven chance of winning. Four things on the board are readings around the optimum, not more optimising: the 0 to 100 share, the fight odds, red's likely six, and the plan stage by stage, which takes one stage at a time (exact, a reading, or greedy).
  • Against the tools. The table below is the study's answer in numbers: each tool's six placed among a board's random sixes - 50 is a random six, 100 beats them all - on the meta, its popularity and strength, and on the team, its synergy and counters, by Blizzard's rates and the wiki and by CounterWatch's 6v6 numbers; and the share of the playbook's rules each six keeps. The charts follow (the results).

The theorem

For every World, board and playbook, and every k ≥ 1, Solver.solve(top=k) (solver.py:248-258) ends in exactly one of three ways:

  1. it returns the first min(k, |LEGAL|) sixes of LEGAL in key order, with their exact scores;
  2. it raises Infeasible, and LEGAL is empty;
  3. it raises Unbounded - past %(NODE_BUDGET)s branches or %(SCORE_BUDGET)s sixes scored in full (solver.py:47-48) - or Superseded, a newer board from the same page, and returns nothing.

It never returns any other list. So the six it returns first, x1, is optimal: for every legal six y, round(score(y), %(SCORE_PLACES)s) ≤ round(score(x1), %(SCORE_PLACES)s), and among the sixes tied there x1 holds the largest draw sum, then the names first in order. Two more things follow.

  • Determinism. The answer depends on the World, the board, the playbook and the weights alone: the scale is drawn from a string seed of the map and the side, the draws are hashes, and the walk's order - the roots by their bounds, the candidates in walk order - changes how fast the answer is found, never the answer.
  • The other exact answers ride the same walk. A full six's rank counts the sixes above it, exactly up to %(RANK_CAP)s; the tie count is exact up to %(RANK_CAP)s within %(TIE_BUDGET)s sixes scored and reads "at least" past either; the fill is the theorem with blue's picks locked; the swap target is the theorem with the keep term; and each stage of the plan is exact given the six it starts from (solver.py:260-285, swaps.py:195-211).

The lemmas

Three lemmas carry the proof: the walk reaches every candidate six once; every bound it asks of a branch is at least every legal six in it; and a branch is dropped only when the sixes already kept beat everything in it. Each opens below.

Lemma 1. The walk covers every candidate six exactly once.
  1. The roster. Each role's candidates are V_r sorted by id (bounds.py:611-617); the space lays the locked picks out first, then each role's candidates, and permutes each role's list into walk order (ranges.py:96-121). A permutation leaves a role's subsets of each size as they were.
  2. The shapes. legal_shapes walks t from 0 to %(MAX_TANKS)s and d from 0 to %(TEAM_SIZE)s − t, s the rest, and keeps a shape only where it seats the locks and every shape limit holds. A shape limit reads only the role counts, the size and the open slots, which every six of a shape shares (shapes.py:57-65), so it keeps or removes whole shapes, never part of one.
  3. The slots. For each shape the walk needs shape_r − |L_r| heroes of each role, skips a shape a role cannot fill, and lays the slots out role by role (solver.py:236-244).
  4. The branching. A slot of role r takes each candidate from position start to the last that leaves room for the role's later slots; the next slot of the same role starts one past it, the next role's at 0 (solver.py:325-334). By induction over the slots, a role's picks are exactly its strictly increasing index sequences of the length needed - each subset once - so a shape holds Π_r C(|V_r|, n_r) leaves. Two shapes differ in their role counts, so the shapes partition CAND, and the sum is the considered the search reports (solver.py:293-295), which the suite holds to a full enumeration's count on every board it checks.
  5. The other limits. At each leaf the six's facts are prepared and every limit it breaks is listed; a six that breaks one is never offered (solver.py:340-347), so the sixes offered are exactly the legal leaves reached.

Lemma 2. Every bound the walk asks of a branch is at least the score of every legal six in it.

A branch holds the picks made so far, the locks among them, and for each open role its candidates from a start on; its completions are exactly the leaves under it (Lemma 1). The walk asks three bounds in turn - the root's, the whole bound, then the fold - each only while those before leave the branch in (bounds.py:411-428). Each is at least the float score of every legal six below the branch, and the branch's tie-break bound is at least the six's draw sum, by these steps:

  1. Intervals. Every expression a rule writes is read over intervals: a value that holds every number between two ends, an exact value, or anything. By induction over the expression, each node's interval holds its value on every completion - sums at the ends, products and quotients at their corners, floor division lowered by one for a quotient rounded up, powers of the whitelisted exponents, comparisons three-valued, and, or and if joining what the branch can still take. A test fails any node or operator without its rule (intervals.py:523-531).
  2. The metrics' ranges. Every team and matchup metric has a range rule that holds its value on every completion - a sum between each open role's smallest and largest parts, a count exactly, a mean between its extremes, a pair sum by half of each open pick's best pairs - widened by a slack of %(SLACK)s a unit of magnitude for another order of summation (ranges.py:1090-1098). A test fails a metric with no rule.
  3. A heuristic's most. A norm is monotone in its metric and a weight is never below 0, so a reward adds at most its weight times the norm at the interval's better end, and a need whose guard may not hold at most 0; a gate the board settles is read once, and a heuristic it shuts adds nothing (bounds.py:87-104).
  4. A scored rule's most. Its weight times the bonus's high end less the penalty's low end, at least 0 where its gate may not hold, and unbounded where an expression's interval is not a number (bounds.py:115-131).
  5. The engine's part. base splits into each pick's own part - its rate and counter terms - and a synergy term over pairs. For the open picks B, the pairs among them sum to half of each pick's pairs with the others, which is at most half of its best ones, for any sign of a pair, so each role's best few worths bound the engine (bounds.py:384-402): 
    base(x) ≤ own(A) + pairs(A) + Σ over the open picks y of worth(y)
worth(y) = own(y) + its pairs with the picks A + half of its m − 1 best pairs among the candidates
  6. The memo and the shape. A term's most depends only on the intervals of the names it reads, so the memo returns what the term would; one on shape-fixed metrics alone is read once a shape; and clearing the memo at %(MEMO_CAP)s entries changes no value (bounds.py:430-471).
  7. The order of addition. The bound adds its pieces in the score's order, and IEEE addition is monotone in both operands, so by induction the float score is at most the float bound (bounds.py:177-184).
  8. The root. The root's bound is the branch's engine part plus each term's most over every six of the branch's shape around the locks - every completion among them (bounds.py:473-479).
  9. The fold. A term that reads only counts of picks, each pick adding 0 or 1, is a function of the counts. For each way the open picks can add to the counts, the fold takes the most worth among the picks that add it and the count terms at those counts - at least every completion's score. It reads %(FOLD_COUNTS)s counts at most, which bounds its cost, not its soundness (bounds.py:483-599).
  10. The tie-break. The draws are whole numbers below 2^%(DRAW_BITS)s, exact in any sum of six; the bound adds the picks' draws and each open role's largest, the exact maximum (bounds.py:601-608).
  11. Limits. A limit the intervals prove false on every completion makes the bound None, and no legal six lies below (bounds.py:438-440).
Lemma 3. A branch is dropped only when k sixes already kept beat every legal six in it.

The search keeps the k best sixes offered so far, by key, so the k-th key only improves (solver.py:126-131). It drops a branch in two cases (solver.py:319-321): a bound is None, and no legal six lies below it; or k sixes are kept and the bound, rounded as a score is, falls below the k-th's score, or meets it while the branch's best tie-break falls below the k-th's (solver.py:117-124). Rounding is monotone, so every legal six below ranks strictly after the k kept. An equal tie-break is never dropped, so the names can still decide.

The proof of the theorem.
  1. Let T be the first n = min(k, |LEGAL|) legal sixes in key order.
  2. Every six of T is reached: were it below a dropped branch, Lemma 3 would put k legal sixes before it, and fewer than k exist.
  3. Every six of T is offered, since it keeps every limit (Lemma 1), and once kept it stays: only k sixes strictly before it could push it out.
  4. The search keeps k at most, so where |LEGAL| ≥ k it ends holding T in order; where |LEGAL| < k no branch is dropped on its score, and it ends holding LEGAL sorted.
  5. Infeasible is raised only where nothing was offered (solver.py:253-257); with nothing kept, no branch is dropped on its score, so every drop was a None bound, and LEGAL is empty.
  6. Unbounded and Superseded stop the search, and no caller passes a part of one off as an answer: blue's optimal passes the refusal on, the fill and the swaps read none, a stage reads unsolved, a tie count reads "at least" and a rank reads unranked (engine.py:409-421, engine.py:529-537, swaps.py:347-353, solver.py:373-376). ∎

What the weights buy

Exactness has a consequence a good guess lacks: each weight pulls the six the way it says.

  • Turning a rule up never makes the optimal six worse on that rule. Write score = F + w · g for one rule of weight w, F free of w. Where w' > w, the two optimal sixes x and x' satisfy F(x) + w · g(x) ≥ F(x') + w · g(x') and F(x') + w' · g(x') ≥ F(x) + w' · g(x); added, they give (w' − w)(g(x') − g(x)) ≥ 0, up to ties at %(SCORE_PLACES)s places. It needs F to hold still as w moves, and it does: the scale reads the sample's metrics, never a weight. Two needs on one guard share a budget, so raising one rescales the other; every shipped need sits alone on its guard.
  • The Meta slider trades in one direction. The same argument with the meta and the engine's part: raising the meta never lowers the optimal six's engine part and never raises its playbook part.
  • With no rule scoring, scaling the meta moves no six, and raising one hero's win rate raises every six that holds it alike, so a hero in the optimal six stays there.

The study checks the first two on every board (the evidence), and the suite holds the first and the third on its synthetic boards (tests/verification/inference/test_stages.py).

What the proof leans on. IEEE-754 doubles rounded to nearest, which is monotone, with Python's round() monotone and math.fsum exact before its one rounding. The range rules matching the metrics' definitions: this one link is held by tests rather than derived - test_bounds checks every metric on every completion of random branches, and the brute force checks real boards - so a metric changed without its rule could break exactness. Every weight within [0, %(WEIGHT_MAX)s], every exponent whole from 0 to 8, and hero names unique. Finite scores, which the World's finite numbers give. And the slack, %(SLACK)s a unit of magnitude, about two orders above the rounding of the few dozen operations it covers: a standard error bound, not a machine-checked one.

Why the plan is greedy, in one example: two phases, and a swap that costs 10 share points. Swapping one hero for another gains 8 in the first phase and 12 in the second. The plan keeps the first hero in the first phase, since 8 is less than 10, and swaps in the second, for a net of 2; swapping in the first phase would net 10. The code says so (swaps.py:40-45).

Nothing hard-coded?

In plain words. The optimiser generalises. The search, the default engine, the scale, the swaps and the playbook's reader name no hero, map, stage or ability, and the roster, the maps, their modes and their stages all come from the database. The team's size is a parameter. Two things are fixed. The roles are three - tank, damage, support - with a cap on tanks, written into the code. And the facts layer, which turns the wiki's kits into numbers, is Overwatch by design: it holds the constants that define its terms and 18 lists of abilities and weapons the wiki has no field for. Those lists are the one real exception to "nothing hard-coded", and they matter.

The audit of 2026-10-05, at commit %(COMMIT)s, searched db/, facts/, inference/, ui/, door/ and orchestrator.py for every hero, with its slug and some 35 former names, map, stage, ability, weapon and perk the database holds: 1,315 hits once 109 false ones were set aside, such as Blizzard the company, which is also Mei's ultimate.

What another team game needs. The same format - six seats, three roles, a cap on the first - needs pulls into the same tables and a playbook of its own, and no change to the engine. Another team size or tank cap is three constants. Other roles are some 30 places in the code. Another kit vocabulary is the facts layer rewritten, with its range rules in step: a test fails a metric without one.

The method

The boards. Every map both data sources know, four red sixes drawn on each from CounterWatch's 6v6 pick rates, and each met at three moments: nothing revealed, one pick per role revealed, all six. Blue picks all six; nothing is banned. The maps are split in two halves within each mode by a seeded shuffle fixed before any result was seen, and the results are read on the test half. It is no clean hold-out: the synergy and counter dials were halved on 2026-10-04 with both halves' results in view, so the train half against the test half is no clean check of generalization. Red is drawn afresh for the study, so no tuning has seen its boards. Further sets repeat the boards of the earlier benchmark, ban the three heroes Countrix fields most, solve on the older capture of the rates, and feed CounterWatch's 6v6 rates to the engine, in memory, never stored. The boards are whole maps, as CounterWatch's numbers per map are.

The arms. Every arm picks a six on every board from data already held; no site is fetched for the study.

  • Countrix, solved exactly, as shipped and in layers: the default engine alone, the engine with the two-of-each-role rule, the playbook alone, and the shipped objective filled slot by slot.
  • CounterWatch's documented team builder, slot by slot on its 6v6 numbers - a reconstruction of its documented model, not the button's output - and the same blend solved exactly over every two-two-two six.
  • Tier lists: the top two per role by win rate on Blizzard's rates and on CounterWatch's, and the documented scores of two meta trackers, HeroStats and OW Hero Stats, run on Blizzard's rates.
  • Counter pickers: per role, the heroes that best answer red's revealed picks, on the wiki's graph and on CounterWatch's duel ratings.
  • The most picked, the top two per role by pick rate on each source, and random sixes, a thousand a board, the 0 of every yardstick.

Editorial tier lists state no model, and the sites that publish them refuse automated readers, so they are left out.

The measures. Each six is placed among its board's random two-two-two sixes - a percentile, ties counted half, 50 a random six - on popularity and strength by Blizzard's rates and by CounterWatch's, on synergy by the wiki and by CounterWatch's duos, and on counters by the wiki and by CounterWatch's matchups and duels, each against red's full six. Two yardsticks score a six whole. CounterWatch's yardstick is its documented team prediction - half counters, three tenths composition, a fifth map advantage, in win-rate points - as a share from the average random six, 0, to the best six it knows, 100, read on its matchups or on its duel ratings. Countrix's scale is its own objective, anchored the same way: 0 the average random six, 100 its optimum. A heuristic's satisfaction runs from 0 to 1.

The statistics. The board is the unit and the map the cluster: a mean over boards, and its 95%% range from drawing the maps again with replacement - the range the average would fall in if the maps were drawn again. A gap between two arms is taken on the same board. No p-values.

What it shows, and what it does not

  • The search is exact: every check above counts its violations, and the count is the claim.
  • Each yardstick favours its home: read a tool's lead on its own numbers as the yardstick's, not the tool's.
  • The layers read what the rules cost on CounterWatch's yardstick: where the default engine alone scores above Countrix as shipped there, the gap is what the playbook's heuristics cost, and where the engine with only the two-of-each-role rule does, what the others cost; the board-by-board table gives each gap with its range. A tier list's lead is not the rules' cost: it differs from Countrix in more than the rules, reading the rates alone, with none of the engine's synergy and counter terms. The rules weigh what CounterWatch's numbers cannot see - a save, a barrier, healing parity, one playstyle - and the rules' table says what each buys and costs.
  • The data swap separates the optimiser from its data: the same engine, fed CounterWatch's rates, is scored on CounterWatch's yardstick beside its own six.
  • It does not show that Countrix wins more games than any tool. No source publishes 6v6 match results, and every yardstick here is a model's reading of the numbers it holds.
